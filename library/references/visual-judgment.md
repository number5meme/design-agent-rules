# Product Context and Visual Judgment Reference

## When to Consult

Use when building a new interface, re-establishing visual direction, or when the user reports that screens feel templated. Small revisions with an established brand follow existing decisions directly.

## How to Borrow

- Derive visuals from the product theme, users, and real content; avoid choosing color and layout from style words alone. When distinctiveness is needed, look for product-specific assets, usage scenarios, materials, content relationships, or real constraints, and turn them into concrete composition, typesetting, or interaction; do not invent brand stories or list cues just to reach a count.
- Type hierarchy, decoration, and motion can serve information, feedback, brand recognition, or emotional expression; keep designs that do work in the actual screen, and remove elements that hinder the goal.
- Action copy describes the action, buttons and results use consistent names, and errors provide a recovery direction.

## Landing Page Information Organization

For landing pages aimed at a specific audience and conversion goal, arrange content by traffic source, visitors' existing knowledge, and main concerns. Entry promises match what the page actually provides, the primary action is clear, and secondary paths are kept as users need; multi-task home pages do not apply a single conversion structure.

Place valid evidence near the corresponding claim, and state key conditions where users need to decide. When evidence is missing, narrow the claim or mark it as pending; do not fabricate customers, reviews, metrics, or offer promises. Choose sections and length by what understanding and decisions need; do not add subtitles, taglines, a fixed number of steps, or FAQs just to complete a structure.

## Typesetting and Reading Rhythm

At the same viewport size, compare text with the same role and use the same size unless the content or context calls for a difference. For a simple page, three or four primary sizes are a useful starting point. Use weight, spacing, or a contrast-checked text color to distinguish emphasis. Preserve readable body text when content grows per [Size and Adaptation](../../standards/defaults.md#size-and-adaptation).

Type character fits the content, language, and use; display headings and body text can use different expression. Check heading line breaks, body reading width, mixed Chinese and English text, number alignment, and the effect after font substitution with real copy; do not decide quality by a fixed number of typefaces or type scale ratios.

Whitespace expresses content relationships: in-group connection, between-group separation, and section pauses can use different scales; adjust with density and reading order, and do not target uniform large spacing or equal-height screens. Consider first-screen focus and the whole-page reading path together, giving headings, images, body text, evidence, and actions clear priorities, and avoid every block competing for attention at the same intensity.

## Color and Composition

On top of existing semantic color rules, check whole-page color area, light/dark distribution, and the relationship between imagery and interface colors. Accent colors carry explicit roles; avoid repeated emphasis that weakens priority; choose specific colors, count, and proportions by content.

When adapting to different sizes, re-judge reading order, heading line breaks, image focal points, information density, and navigation, rather than only shrinking or stacking. Adjust layout where content is crowded or relationships break, keeping key information and actions; do not cut arbitrarily for adaptation.

## Original Expression and Trade-offs

Build distinctiveness from real content, brand story, assets, and interaction purpose. When judging templating, you may temporarily ignore the logo and name and observe whether content organization and presentation still reflect the project's character; this is only a diagnostic cue and does not require ordinary controls or navigation to be deliberately different.

When comparing multiple directions, check whether differences show in information organization, composition, type roles, density, or asset expression, not just recoloring and renaming; each direction still meets the shared content and functional requirements. Do not force a number of directions or of difference dimensions; when the user only asks for a color comparison, other dimensions can stay the same.

When polishing, check what role each label, container, card, highlight, and motion plays: would removing it lose understanding, grouping, operation boundaries, feedback, brand recognition, or emotional expression? After removing elements with no role or duplicates, re-view the actual screen to confirm information is complete, the subject is clear, and rhythm and layering are still sufficient; do not treat emptying content or weakening presentation as simplification.

Judge the expressive benefit of a technique together with loading, reading, operation, and maintenance costs. Brand showcases and high-frequency workspaces can make different trade-offs; large type, minimalism, generous whitespace, dark themes, gradients, 3D, or scroll effects are chosen by purpose, not used as quality thresholds.

## Application

In the project design record, state key rationale, design actions, and check methods. Arrange the workflow by task size, and choose color, type, and appearance by brand, content, and platform; provide an entry point in empty states only when a reasonable next step exists.

## Testing a Proposed Direction

Before building a new visual direction, sketch the intended hierarchy, layout, type and palette in the current design record. Try the same plan against a similar brief with a different audience or purpose. If it fits unchanged, identify which choices the real content justifies and revise those that lack a reason.

Look for decoration that implies a relationship the content does not have. Numbered steps imply a sequence. Repeated uppercase labels should distinguish meaningful groups. Identical rounded cards and shadows should reflect equal roles. Treat a cream background with a serif headline as a common default. Keep it when the brief, content, or brand supports that choice. Use these cues to ask what each choice does, and retain choices supported by the content or agreed brand.
